Case study · Social Media Design

Social media kits — one brand voice, everywhere.

Facebook covers for six very different brands, plus a full Instagram awareness campaign for a nonprofit — each one designed to match the client's world, not a template.

Role
Brand & Social Media Designer
Tools
Photoshop · Illustrator
Deliverables
FB Covers · IG Post Kit
Clients
6 Brands
Industries
Beauty · Fashion · Coaching · Nonprofit

The Brief

Social media is often the first thing a potential client sees — and for small businesses, the Facebook cover and Instagram feed usually have to do the job a full website would. This case study rounds up Facebook cover designs across six brands spanning beauty, fashion, coaching, and photography, plus a closer look at a complete Instagram campaign kit built for a women's safety nonprofit — showing the same designer adapting tone, color, and typography to fit six completely different audiences.

Facebook Covers

Six brands, six visual languages.

Each cover had to work as a first impression — legible at a glance, on brand with the client's existing colors or photography, and distinct enough to stand out in a crowded feed.

House of Intrigue

A gold script wordmark paired with an eye and brush icon, set against soft tulip photography. The client wanted something that felt elegant and feminine without leaning on generic beauty industry clichés like flat lays or product shots. This cover became the anchor for her entire page redesign, and she later commissioned a matching profile icon to keep the look consistent.

JB Luxe

A bold pink monogram sits over a gold foil bordered interior shot, reinforcing the brand line "When there's beauty, there's luxury." The client specifically asked for something that would photograph well as a thumbnail preview, since most of her followers browse Facebook on mobile. The gold border was added late in the process specifically to solve that visibility problem.

Mali Bootique

A clean, minimal cover built entirely around the brand's circular badge logo. Rather than adding extra graphics or photography, the design leans on generous white space and lets the mark carry the whole composition. This approach suited a boutique brand that wanted to feel premium and uncluttered rather than busy.

Saree Reveling

A professional headshot placed over a subtle network node pattern, positioning a business coach and strategist as connected and credible. The node pattern was a deliberate metaphor for her coaching philosophy around building networks and relationships, something she mentioned in the original brief.

SoulFace Studio

A moody portrait paired with a clean wordmark and a wing and silhouette icon. Since this cover was for a photography studio, the design had to showcase photographic quality itself, so the client's own work was used as the hero image rather than a stock alternative.

Campaign Deep-Dive

Baji Foundation — a full awareness campaign.

Baji Foundation needed a Facebook cover and a full set of Instagram ready posts for a women's safety and dignity campaign — every piece had to carry the same teal-and-charcoal palette, dot-pattern texture, and documentary style photography so the campaign read as one voice across a donor's entire feed.

Facebook Cover

"Empowering Women. Restoring Dignity." — black-and-white documentary photography paired with the campaign's teal accent color.

Design Approach

Every cover started with the client's existing brand cues — a logo, a color, sometimes just a vibe in a brief — and built outward in Photoshop, using real photography wherever it strengthened the message (tulips for a beauty brand, a coach's own headshot, documentary photos of the women Baji Foundation serves). Illustrator handled the icon work and pattern elements, like the node network behind Saree Reveling and the dot-texture backdrop for the Baji Foundation series.

For the nonprofit kit specifically, the goal was repetition with variation — the same teal, the same dot pattern, the same photography style across all eight posts, but each with its own headline and call-to-action, so the campaign felt like a coordinated push rather than eight unrelated graphics.
